    Brewing Canada's beers, from New France to today

    Foreword

    Introduction

    When I started this project, I wasn't trying to write the history of beer in Canada, or of its brewers. Others have done that, and done it brilliantly. I wanted instead to revisit the history of the beers themselves: to trace how the styles that quenched people's thirst here evolved over time. By brewing these beers, I wanted to bring their spirit back to life and imagine what generations of drinkers might have tasted.

    The further back we go, the harder it is to recreate the profile of yesterday's drinks with any certainty. Techniques have changed, ingredients are no longer produced the same way, and written records are fragmentary at best. Claiming historical accuracy is nearly impossible.

    I'm not a historian. My goal is rather to make beers that taste good to today's palates. Each recipe therefore says clearly what comes from the sources and what is interpretation.

    If you're already a seasoned homebrewer, skip straight to the recipes. If you've never brewed, or you're just starting, take the time to read the first chapters. You'll see: it's not that complicated.

    How to use this site

    • The recipes adjust. The My equipment panel lets you choose the batch size, your system's efficiency and the units (metric or imperial). Every quantity is recalculated.
    • The numbers are calculated. Gravities, bitterness (IBU), colour and alcohol are computed live from the ingredients, using the same formulas as brewing software.
    • Sources are given. Every chapter and every recipe points to the documents consulted. The full list is at the end.

    Grab your kettles and follow me on this little trip through time!

    Chapter 1

    Humans and alcohol: hooked from the cradle

    Let's start with a short detour to place beer in the history of humanity, no less. In short, we've been "having a drink" since our species was in its infancy. As soon as we learned to grow grain, we turned it into "liquid bread".

    Before agriculture

    In fact, we know very little. Alcoholic drinks very likely existed before agriculture: a beehive left in water for a few weeks, and there's mead; fruit juice left to itself, and there's wine. Humans probably met alcohol long before they understood how to make it.

    Some archaeologists even think beer may have motivated the cultivation of cereals. In 2018, a Stanford University team found traces of brewing about 13,000 years old in Raqefet Cave, Israel, left by the Natufians, semi-sedentary hunter-gatherers. That is almost as old as the oldest known bread remains, found in Jordan and dated to about 14,000 years ago.

    The first civilizations

    In China, at Jiahu, 9,000-year-old jars held a fermented drink of rice, honey and fruit. In Mesopotamia, beer is everywhere: people drink it through straws to avoid the grain residue, workers are paid in beer rations, and the Hymn to Ninkasi, the Sumerian goddess of beer, inscribed on a tablet around 1800 BCE, describes how it is made. Beer bread (bappir) is both a food and an ingredient: bread and beer are two sides of the same activity. In Egypt, the builders of the Giza pyramids also received a daily ration.

    According to each continent's resources

    Everywhere, the kind of alcohol depends on what grows:

    • Greece and Rome: vine country. They drink wine and see beer as a drink for "barbarians". Tacitus marvels at the Germans who drink a liquid made from barley.
    • Northern Europe and the British Isles: the Gauls drink cervoise (from the Latin cervesia, hence cerveza). In the Middle Ages, beer is flavoured with gruit, a blend of herbs, until hops take over. One of the earliest mentions of their use comes from the abbey of Corbie, in Picardy, in 822.
    • Asia: grain isn't malted; moulds (Japanese koji, Chinese qu) turn rice starch into sugar. This is the sake family.
    • Africa: sorghum and millet beers, often cloudy and tart, and palm wine.
    • The Americas: corn chicha in the Andes (traditionally the grain is chewed: saliva converts the starch), pulque from agave sap in Mexico, Maya balché made from bark and honey.

    What about the Indigenous peoples of the Northeast?

    North of Mexico, alcohol production before the arrival of Europeans was rare. In the American Southwest, the Apache brewed tiswin, a corn beer, and the Tohono O'odham made a wine from saguaro fruit. Researchers have analysed potsherds about 800 years old from New Mexico looking for traces of corn beer, but the results remain inconclusive.

    In the Northeast, among the Iroquoians, the Algonquians or the Innu, no alcoholic drink is known, even though the Wendat and the Haudenosaunee grew corn. Their drinks were infusions and decoctions, some with an important medicinal role: one of these decoctions, annedda, saved Jacques Cartier's crew from scurvy in 1536.

    Alcohol therefore arrived with the Europeans, above all brandy, the currency of the fur trade. The damage it caused in Indigenous communities led to a long conflict between the Church, led by Bishop Laval, and the merchants. In 1678, an assembly of notables, the "Brandy Parliament", came out in favour of continuing the brandy trade.

    Distillation, briefly

    Fermentation rarely goes past 15% alcohol: beyond that, the yeast dies. To go further, you have to distil, heating the liquid and collecting the alcohol vapours, which boil off before water. Alchemists of the Arab world perfected the still; in the Middle Ages, European aqua vitae was first a medicine. By the 17th century, wine brandy, easier to ship than wine, had become a global trade good.

    Alcohol and ritual

    Almost every culture has given alcohol a sacred role: libations to the gods, communion wine, harvest-festival beer, wedding toasts. Drunkenness opens a door to something else, and societies have always tried to control it.

    Waves of prohibition

    Sometimes that control takes the form of a ban. In the 19th century, temperance movements swept across North America, driven by the Protestant churches and, in Quebec, by the Catholic clergy and its black temperance crosses. In Canada, almost every province adopted prohibition during the First World War. We'll come back to it in the chapter on Americanization: it was a turning point for beer styles.

    Chapter 2

    How beer is made

    Alcohol exists thanks to a marvellous organism: yeast. A microscopic fungus that eats sugar and gives off alcohol and carbon dioxide. Everything else is the art of preparing the right meal for it, then letting it work in good conditions.

    A bit of science

    For each molecule of glucose, yeast produces two molecules of ethanol and two of CO2. That's alcoholic fermentation. Along the way it also produces hundreds of aroma compounds: fruity esters (banana, pear), spicy phenols (clove), higher alcohols. Each strain has its signature. That's why the same recipe fermented with an English yeast or a Belgian yeast gives two very different beers.

    The basic rule is simple: if there's a source of sugar, there can be alcohol. Grapes and apples already contain sugar. So does honey. Cereals, however, contain starch: long chains of sugar that yeast can't eat. They have to be cut up first.

    Malting: preparing the grain

    Malting means letting barley sprout, then drying it. As it germinates, the grain makes enzymes (amylases) able to turn its starch into sugar. Drying stops germination and, depending on the temperature, gives the malt its colour and flavour: biscuit, caramel, coffee, chocolate. Canada is now one of the world's leading producers of malting barley; Canada Malting Co. was founded in Toronto in 1902, and small craft maltsters have broadened the choice in Quebec since the 2010s.

    It all comes down to controlling each step

    Extracting the sugars: the mash

    Crushed malt is steeped in hot water, between 62 and 70 °C, for about an hour. The enzymes cut up the starch. At 62–65 °C they mostly make simple sugars that ferment easily: the beer will be dry. At 68–70 °C they leave more complex sugars: the beer will have more body. The sweet liquid you get is called wort.

    The role of hops

    Hops are a climbing plant, a cousin of cannabis. Their cones contain alpha acids, which become bitter when boiled, and essential oils, which provide aroma. Hops are also a preservative: that's why they replaced gruit. The longer they boil, the more bitterness they give and the less aroma is left. Added at the end, or in the fermenter (dry hopping), they add aroma without bitterness. Bitterness is measured in IBU (International Bitterness Units).

    Why boil

    The boil, usually 60 to 90 minutes, does several jobs: it sterilizes the wort, extracts bitterness from the hops, coagulates proteins (the "hot break") that would make the beer hazy, and drives off unwanted compounds such as DMS, which tastes like canned corn.

    Chilling

    The wort must be cooled as fast as possible, below 25 °C, to reduce the risk of contamination and let the yeast survive. A copper immersion chiller or an ice bath in the sink will do.

    Pitching the yeast

    The yeast is added to the cooled, well-aerated wort (yeast needs oxygen to multiply at the start). A local anecdote: one of the largest yeast companies in the world, Lallemand, was born in Montréal in 1915. Many of the dry yeasts suggested here come from them.

    Controlling fermentation

    Temperature is the most important variable. Ale yeasts work around 16–22 °C, lager yeasts around 8–13 °C. Too warm, and yeast produces heavy alcohols and solvent aromas; too cold, and it goes to sleep. An ale fermentation takes one to two weeks.

    Carbonating

    To get bubbles, a little sugar is added at bottling. The remaining yeast ferments it inside the closed bottle, and the CO2 dissolves into the beer. Carbonation is measured in volumes of CO2: about 2.0 for an English ale, 2.5 for a blonde, 3 and up for a Belgian beer. Each recipe's calculator gives the amount of sugar to use.

    Conditioning

    After fermentation, beer benefits from resting: a few days for a blonde, months for a barley wine. Flavours round out, yeast drops, the beer clears. Lagers are kept near 0 °C for weeks (lager means "to store" in German).

    Measuring: the hydrometer

    The hydrometer is the brewer's best friend. It measures the density of the wort, and so its sugar content. You measure the original gravity (OG) before fermentation and the final gravity (FG) at the end. The difference lets you calculate the alcohol. This tool has a Canadian story: John Molson owned John Richardson's Theoretic Hints on an Improved Practice of Brewing (1777), one of the first books to argue for using the saccharometer in brewing.

    Cleaning, sanitizing and sterilizing

    This is the golden rule. Everything that touches the wort after the boil must be clean, then sanitized. We distinguish:

    • Cleaning: removing visible dirt (hot water, unscented soap, or a percarbonate cleaner such as PBW or unscented OxiClean).
    • Sanitizing: killing most microbes on a clean surface (Star San or a no-rinse iodine sanitizer).
    • Sterilizing: killing everything, normally with heat. The boil takes care of the wort; for everything else, sanitizing is enough.

    Stack the odds in your favour: most failed beers are caused by contamination or a fermentation that ran too warm, not by a bad recipe.

    Chapter 3

    A simple method for beginners: brew in a bag

    The method used here is called BIAB, for Brew In A Bag: you brew in a single kettle, with the grain in a large mesh bag. No lauter tun, no complicated sparging. It's the simplest way to make real all-grain beer.

    Essential equipment

    • A large kettle. For a 19-litre BIAB batch you need at least 30 litres, ideally 36 to 40 litres (8 to 10 gallons), because all the water and the grain go in at once. With a 16 to 20 litre pot, brew 8 to 10 litre batches: the calculator adjusts everything.
    • A fine-mesh bag (homebrew shops sell BIAB bags sized to kettles).
    • A thermometer, ideally instant-read, like a meat thermometer.
    • A large spoon in stainless steel or food-grade plastic.
    • A large colander (a pasta colander will do) to drain the bag.
    • A fermenter: a 23-litre (6-gallon) carboy or fermenting bucket, with an airlock.
    • A bottling bucket with a spigot.
    • Bottles (not twist-off) and caps, with a capper.
    • A siphon or auto-siphon with a few feet of silicone tubing.
    • A hydrometer and its test jar.
    • A no-rinse sanitizer (Star San, iodophor).
    • A kitchen scale accurate to the gram, for hops.

    Brew day, step by step

    1. Heat the water. Put the full volume of water given by the recipe in the kettle and heat it to strike temperature (the calculator gives it: it's a little higher than the target, because the cold grain will lower it).
    2. Mash in. Put the bag in the kettle, pour in the crushed grain and stir well to break up clumps. Check the temperature: it should be within a degree or two of the target. Cover, wrap the kettle in a blanket and wait an hour.
    3. Pull the bag. Lift the bag, let it drain in the colander over the kettle and squeeze it gently. Every litre you recover is extra sugar.
    4. Boil. Bring the wort to a boil. Add hops on the recipe's schedule ("60 min" means 60 minutes before the end of the boil). Leave the lid off.
    5. Chill. At the end, cool the wort below 25 °C as fast as possible. From this point on, everything that touches it must be sanitized.
    6. Measure and transfer. Take the original gravity. Pour the wort into the fermenter, splashing it to aerate.
    7. Pitch the yeast. Sprinkle the dry yeast (or rehydrate it in a little lukewarm water), seal with the airlock and put the fermenter somewhere at the right temperature.
    8. Wait. When the gravity is stable two days in a row, fermentation is done.
    9. Bottle. Dissolve the priming sugar in a little boiled water, pour it into the bottling bucket, siphon the beer on top without splashing, then fill and cap the bottles. Wait two weeks at room temperature.

    How recipe scaling works

    All recipes are written for a reference volume (usually 19 litres into the fermenter) and an efficiency of 70%. The My equipment panel lets you change both. Here's what happens behind the scenes.

    Volume

    If you go from 19 to 10 litres, every ingredient is multiplied by 10 ÷ 19. Gravity, bitterness, colour and alcohol don't change: it's the same beer, just smaller.

    Efficiency

    Efficiency is the share of the sugar in the grain that you actually get into the fermenter. The rest stays in the wet grain or at the bottom of the kettle. A BIAB setup usually runs between 65 and 75%, depending on how fine the crush is, how long the mash lasts and how you squeeze the bag.

    If your efficiency is lower than the recipe's, you need more grain for the same gravity. The calculator therefore multiplies the mashed grains by 70 ÷ your efficiency. At 60% you need about 17% more grain; at 80%, about 12% less. Sugars, honey, molasses and juice don't go through the mash: they only change with volume.

    Hops and the rest

    Hops follow the volume. Bitterness is recalculated with the Tinseth formula, the one most brewing software uses. If your hops' alpha acid differs from the recipe's, adjust the amount in proportion: a 6% hop instead of 4% calls for two-thirds of the quantity.

    Water

    In BIAB, all the water goes into the kettle at the start. The calculator adds up the final volume, what the grain holds back (about 1 litre per kilo, bag squeezed), what evaporates during the boil (at your boil-off rate) and what stays at the bottom of the kettle (trub and hops, about 1.5 litres).

    Measure my efficiency

    After your first batch, enter what you measured. The calculation assumes a typical base malt.

    Reading a recipe

    Each recipe card gives the key values up top: original and final gravity, alcohol, bitterness (IBU) and colour (SRM, with a swatch of the approximate hue). Then come the ingredients, the mash, the boil schedule, the yeast and the fermentation. Difficulty goes from one to three mugs: one for a beginner's recipe, three for those that need precise temperature control or patience.

    Chapter 4

    Canada's beers, from then to now

    The aim of what follows is to "live" the history of Canada's beers by recreating them. Some are easy to make, others more complex. I've taken some artistic licence: it's almost impossible to faithfully reproduce a historical beer, because too much information is missing. The goal is a drinkable beer that tells the story of its time.

    This history falls into four broad eras. Each one corresponds to a different economic world: who drinks, who brews, with what, and for whom.

    1608–1800Traditional brewsHome beer, bouillon, spruce beer, cider. People brew at home with what they have. 1786–1915The industrial eraMolson, Labatt, Keith, Oland. Porter, pale ale, IPA, then the arrival of lager. 1916–1980Americanization and uniformityProhibition, consolidation, three big brewers and one taste. 1982–todayThe craft beer revivalHorseshoe Bay, Boréale, Unibroue, then the boom of the 2010s.

    The same logic runs through all four eras: beer follows the economy. In the 17th century it depends on harvests and household labour, mostly women's. In the 19th it follows the railway, steam and refrigeration. In the 20th, advertising and economies of scale reward very large companies and a single product. At the end of the century, wealthier and more curious consumers, and looser regulation, open the door to the small players again.

    Chapter 5 · 1608–1800

    Traditional home brews

    Under the French regime, beer is first of all a household matter. People brew it at home, the way they bake bread, with the barley, wheat or oats of the harvest, and drink it young. Alongside it they drink wine and brandy imported from France, cider, and two drinks very much of this place: bouillon and spruce beer.

    The first brewers

    Most immigrants came from western France (Normandy, Perche, Poitou, Aunis, Saintonge) and the Paris region: cider and wine country far more than beer country. The true brewing regions, Picardy and Artois, supplied only a small share. Beer here would therefore be mostly domestic and practical, made with whatever grain was available. As early as 1617, Louis Hébert sowed barley in Québec. In 1620, the Recollect Denis Jamet wrote: "We have enough grain to make bread and beer". In 1622, Champlain mentions a brewing kettle in Québec "holding nearly a tun". The Jesuits brewed for their community from 1635, and in 1646–1647 Brother Ambroise made beer for the fathers, who had lost their wine ration.

    In the 1640s, the Communauté des habitants ran a brewery in Québec; in 1648, Jacques Boisdon obtained the exclusive right to open an inn and serve its beer. Throughout the French regime, some twenty official brewers worked in Québec, Trois-Rivières and Montréal. According to historian Catherine Ferland, their wives, often from Normandy, held much of the know-how, without receiving the title.

    Jean Talon's Brasserie du Roy

    Intendant Jean Talon arrived in 1665 with a big plan: to make the colony something more than a fur-trading post. From 1668 he had a large brewery built in Québec, at the foot of the cliff. His goals were clear: use surplus grain, cut imports of alcohol and the consumption of brandy, and export to the West Indies. In his letters, he hoped beer would "spur the habitant to work at cultivating the land". In 1671 he wrote to the king that the brewery was finished and could produce up to 4,000 barrels a year.

    The project was too big for the colony. The local market was tiny, settlers brewed at home, and brandy remained more profitable. The brewery closed only a few years after it opened (around 1675, according to The Canadian Encyclopedia); the building was sold and turned into the intendant's palace. History has a sense of irony: in 1853, Joseph Knight Boswell's brewery bought the land and moved in. Renamed the Dow brewery in 1952, it made beer on the same spot until 1968. Archaeological digs began in 1983, and the remains of Talon's brewery are now showcased at the Îlot des Palais in Québec City.

    The Conquest and the Loyalists

    After the Conquest of 1760, the colony passed into British hands. Soldiers and merchants from the British Isles brought their taste for ale, porter and stout, and commercial brewing picked up again. From 1783, the Loyalists fleeing the American Revolution added the habits of the English colonies: molasses small beer, military spruce beer, pumpkin beer.

    Indigenous influences

    The greatest Indigenous contribution to colonial drinking was indirect but vital: knowledge of conifers as a cure for scurvy. Spruce beer, sweetened with molasses or maple syrup, became the everyday drink of the Canadiens in the 18th century, to the point that Pehr Kalm wrote in 1749 that brewing beer from malt was not yet customary. Maple syrup and sugar, learned from Indigenous peoples, also sweetened plenty of drinks.

    Chapter 6 · 1786–1915

    British and German influences, and industrialization

    In the 19th century, Canadian beer becomes an industry. The brewers come from England, Scotland and Ireland, and brew what London drinks: ale, porter, stout. Then come German immigrants, the steam engine, the railway and ice, and with them, lager.

    John Molson and the English tradition

    John Molson arrived in Montréal in 1782, at 18. In 1786 he brewed his first beer in a small brewery on the riverbank. To learn, he studied John Richardson's Theoretic Hints on an Improved Practice of Brewing (1777), a modern manual that championed measurement and method. Molson is today the oldest brewery in North America still operating.

    Others followed: Thomas Dunn at La Prairie (1790), then William Dow, who made his Montréal brewery Molson's great rival in Quebec; Alexander Keith in Halifax (1820); John Sleeman in Ontario (1834); Thomas Carling (1840) and John Kinder Labatt (1847) in London; Susannah Oland in Dartmouth (1867); Eugene O'Keefe in Toronto (1862). By 1860 the country had more than 150 breweries, almost all of them local.

    A beer economy

    From the 1850s, the railway changed everything. A brewery in London or Montréal could now sell its beer hundreds of kilometres away. From the 1870s, refrigerated cars kept beer cold and fresh; in 1886, Canada's first mechanical refrigeration system started running in Montréal. The big breweries invested in steam, cellars, bottles and brands. The small ones closed or were bought out.

    Styles followed British fashion: porter first, then pale ale and IPA from mid-century. In 1893–1894, IPA made up 74% of Labatt's output. Canadian brewers adapted these beers to their ingredients: Ontario six-row barley, richer in protein than English barley, and, more and more, corn.

    German immigration and lager

    German immigrants settled mostly in Ontario, in Waterloo County, and brought lager with them. George Rebscher is credited with the first lager brewed in North America, in 1837, in Berlin (now Kitchener). But lager needs cold: cellars, ice cut from lakes in winter, then refrigeration machines. It slowly gained ground in Ontario and the West, while Quebec and the Maritimes stayed loyal to ale.

    A question of barley

    For decades, Canadian malting barley was mostly six-row barley, such as the OAC 21 variety bred at the agricultural college in Guelph. Richer in enzymes than two-row barley, it easily converts unmalted grains such as corn or rice. That's one reason North American beers became paler and lighter than their European cousins.

    Chapter 7 · 1916–1980

    Americanization and uniformity

    In the 20th century, Canadian beer becomes an industrial feat: a stable, uniform product delivered everywhere with the same taste. The price is diversity. By 1980, a handful of companies sell almost all the country's beer, and those beers all taste alike.

    Prohibition

    Carried by the temperance movements and the war effort, prohibition swept almost the whole country during the First World War: Nova Scotia in 1916 (until 1930), Ontario from 1916 to 1927, Manitoba, Alberta, Saskatchewan, New Brunswick, British Columbia and Yukon. Prince Edward Island holds the record: 1901 to 1948. From 1918 to 1920, a federal measure capped "temperance" beer at 2.5% alcohol.

    Quebec took its own path. On April 10, 1919, a referendum asked whether the sale of light beer, cider and wine should remain legal. "Yes" won with 78.6%. In 1921, the province created the Commission des liqueurs, ancestor of the SAQ: the state became the seller of alcohol rather than banning it. Quebec became a destination for thirsty Americans, and Montréal earned its reputation as a party town.

    The effects on the industry were huge. Between 1915 and 1920, the number of Canadian breweries fell from 118 to 69. Those that survived did so by exporting, often by roundabout routes, to the United States, where prohibition lasted from 1920 to 1933.

    Consolidation

    In the 1930s and 1940s, Toronto businessman E. P. Taylor bought some thirty breweries, closed most of them and cut about 150 brands down to eight. His empire became Canadian Breweries Limited, then Carling O'Keefe. In 1945, Canada still had 61 breweries; by 1962, Labatt, Molson and Canadian Breweries produced about 95% of the beer sold in the country. A federal inquiry called Taylor's strategy "predatory", to no effect.

    In this market, companies competed on brand and advertising more than on taste. Breweries aimed for the beer that would put off the fewest people: pale, light, barely bitter, very fizzy, served very cold. Corn and rice lightened the recipe and cost less than malt.

    The East holds out

    Eastern Canada stayed attached to ale much longer than the rest of North America. In Quebec, taverns poured Molson Export, Dow, Labatt 50, Black Horse or O'Keefe. But these ales changed too: paler and lighter, cold-conditioned, they ended up resembling lagers.

    The Dow affair

    Between August 1965 and April 1966, 48 heavy beer drinkers were hospitalized in Québec City with severe heart failure; 20 of them died. All drank Dow, to which the brewery had been adding cobalt sulphate since July 1965 to stabilize the foam. Dow's sales collapsed and the brand never recovered. The episode shows how much industrial beer had become a technical product, tuned by chemistry.

    In the 1970s and 1980s, lager won everywhere. In 1980, Labatt obtained the licence to brew Budweiser in Canada; "dry", "ice" and "light" beers multiplied. It was against this landscape that the craft brewing movement was born.

    Chapter 8 · 1982 to today

    The craft beer revival

    In the early 1980s, only about ten independent breweries remained in Canada, and three big companies controlled 96% of the market. Forty years later, the country has more than 1,100 breweries. In between came a revolution started by homebrewers, pubs and a few stubborn people.

    The American spark

    In the United States, homebrewing was legalized in 1978. A generation of enthusiasts went commercial: New Albion in California (1976), Sierra Nevada (1980), and San Francisco's Anchor, saved by Fritz Maytag in 1965, which revived forgotten styles. That same year, 1978, in Canada, brewer Frank Appleton published an article in Harrowsmith magazine that set many readers dreaming.

    The Canadian pioneers

    In 1979, a strike at Carling left the Troller Pub in Horseshoe Bay, near Vancouver, without beer. Its owner, John Mitchell, teamed up with Frank Appleton to brew his own beer on old dairy equipment. The Horseshoe Bay Brewery opened in 1982: the first modern craft brewery in Canada. In 1984, Granville Island Brewing opened in Vancouver and Brick Brewing in Waterloo, Ontario. Wellington County and Upper Canada followed in Ontario.

    In Quebec

    Quebec followed closely. In 1982, Brasserie Massawippi, in the Eastern Townships, distributed a first craft ale. In the mid-1980s, the provincial regulator allowed establishments to brew and sell on site: the Golden Lion in Lennoxville poured its first pint on July 1, 1986, and Le Cheval Blanc opened the same year on Ontario Street in Montréal. Les Brasseurs du Nord (1987) with its Boréale Rousse, then McAuslan (1989) with its St-Ambroise, put craft beer on the shelves of the corner dépanneurs.

    In 1990, André Dion and Serge Racine founded Unibroue in Chambly. Their bet was huge: to set the bold character of Belgian beers, fruity, spicy, often strong, against smooth, uniform lagers. Blanche de Chambly (1992), Maudite (1992) and then La Fin du Monde (1994) proved there was room for these beers. Quebec thus developed a brewing identity of its own, more Belgian and French than British or American. The Mondial de la bière, launched in Montréal in 1994, became its showcase.

    Bust, then boom

    The early 1990s were hard: many new breweries went under, often for lack of capital or consistent quality. Writer Stephen Beaumont saw it as a shake-out of the weakest players. Growth picked up again at the end of the decade (Dieu du Ciel! opened in Montréal in 1998), then exploded from 2005–2010 on. In 2001, Canada had 163 breweries; in 2023 it had 1,185, a peak. Since then the number has slipped a little: 1,112 breweries in 2025, 259 of them in Quebec, and beer consumption per person has fallen 16% since 2019.

    The choice has become so diverse it's dizzying, and quality varies. Some styles have been revived, like porters, stouts and sours; others invented, like the NEIPA. The big breweries responded by buying craft breweries: Unibroue (Sleeman, then Sapporo), Creemore Springs and Granville Island (Molson).

    In closing

    Conclusion

    In four centuries, beer here has taken quite a journey. It started in the kitchens of the habitantes, industrialized with the English brewers, became uniform to the rhythm of advertising and mergers, then began to flourish again in pubs and small breweries.

    Brewing these recipes, you quickly notice that every era made the beer its means allowed. The molasses in spruce beer is the West Indies trade. The corn in lagers is six-row barley and economies of scale. The American hops in modern IPAs are globalized agriculture and drinkers willing to pay for flavour. Beer tells the story of its economy as surely as a history book.

    The circle closes nicely, too. Today, Quebec craft breweries are once again making spruce beer, cider, farmhouse beers and maple syrup beers. They're not trying to reproduce the past exactly, any more than this project is. They're looking for what the habitants of 1660 were looking for: a good beer, made here, with what's at hand.

    Cheers!

    Appendix

    Short glossary

    Attenuation
    The share of the sugar the yeast has eaten. 75% apparent attenuation means the gravity has dropped by three-quarters.
    BIAB
    Brew In A Bag: all-grain brewing in a single kettle, with the grain in a bag.
    Dry hopping
    Adding hops to the fermenter, for aroma without bitterness.
    Efficiency
    The share of the grain's sugar that actually ends up in the fermenter. See "How recipe scaling works".
    First wort hopping
    Hops added to the wort before the boil. It gives a smoother bitterness.
    IBU
    International Bitterness Units: milligrams of iso-alpha acids per litre. A measure of bitterness.
    Lager and ale
    Two families of yeast. Ale ferments warm (16–22 °C) and gives fruity aromas; lager ferments cold (8–13 °C) and gives a cleaner beer.
    Mash
    Steeping crushed malt in hot water (62–70 °C) to turn starch into sugar.
    Original (OG) and final gravity (FG)
    Hydrometer readings before and after fermentation.
    Party-gyle
    A technique that draws several beers of different strengths from one mash.
    SRM and EBC
    Beer colour scales. 2 SRM: pale straw; 10: amber; 20: brown; 40 and up: black. EBC ≈ SRM × 1.97.
    Whirlpool
    Hops added after the boil, around 80 °C, for aroma with little bitterness.
    Wort
    The sweet liquid obtained after the mash, before fermentation.
